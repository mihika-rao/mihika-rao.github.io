The question behind the work

What are we
actually teaching?

My route here runs through astronomy, classroom teaching, education research, and learning-product analytics. Across those settings, I keep returning to the same problem: an observation becomes useful only when we understand what it does—and does not—tell us.

A wrong answer might reveal a misconception. It might also reveal a vague question, an unfamiliar script, or a perfectly reasonable interpretation the answer key forgot to include.

I speak Kannada, Hindi, and Marathi, am beginning Malayalam, and have a 1,000-plus-day French streak on Duolingo. That gives me a personal reason to care. It does not make one speaker’s experience a substitute for a language educator or a community of speakers.

What is complete?

Three browser-local demos, 24 practice/case items, six deliberately constructed review fixtures, a proposed rubric, and test plans. The examples were drafted with AI assistance and checked against the references below. No independent linguistic sign-off, participant study, or production deployment is claimed.

Project 01 / Curriculum + context-sensitive feedback

You, in Context.

English gives you “you.” Hindi asks who you’re talking to.

The design problem

A pronoun-only translation prompt can hide the information needed to answer it. This prototype supplies a relationship, a setting, or an expressed preference before asking for a choice.

Hindi has distinctions among आप, तुम, तू. Their use involves social meaning as well as grammar. UT Austin’s Hamari Boli treats choosing among these forms as a context-dependent issue, not a simple word substitution. [1]

The curriculum decision

Begin with a respectful first meeting, introduce an explicitly agreed familiar form, then examine an intimate use. Follow with the standard agreement patterns taught here. Finish with new situations and a question about missing context.

The course would teach a usable starting convention while allowing learners to discover that real speakers differ. The answer key describes the target of a particular exercise; it is not a verdict on every regional variety.

LearnerEnglish-speaking adult beginner. Transliteration available; no reading prerequisite for the first scenarios.
Learning objectiveUse stated context to select a defensible form, then match it with the taught agreement pattern.
Scope12 cards in three stages. Standard patterns for this prototype; not a complete account of Hindi-Urdu usage.

Proposed learner study and next decisions

Hypothesis: context-first practice will help learners explain their choice on an unseen situation, beyond memorizing a pronoun label.

Start with 5–8 adult beginners for formative usability sessions. Record prior Hindi exposure and script familiarity. Use distinct pre- and post-task scenarios, ask learners to explain one choice, and code errors as context, agreement, reading, or item ambiguity. Offer a delayed follow-up after 48–72 hours. Do not claim causality or population-level effectiveness from this small convenience sample.

Next decision: revise any item whose key cannot be defended by the supplied context. If learners repeat the right word but cannot explain its use, add contrasting situations before adding more vocabulary. A later powered comparison would need independent item review, parallel test forms, random assignment, and a preregistered analysis.

Project 02 / Script learning + calibrated support

A Small Sign,
a New Sound.

A learner can recognize a word without knowing how to read it.

The design problem

If every task uses familiar words, a correct response may come from memory rather than decoding. This prototype holds the consonant steady, varies a vowel sign, and then changes the consonant to check transfer.

Devanagari uses independent vowels and dependent vowel signs. In these exercises, ा, ि, ी produce ā, i, and ī. The short-i sign is displayed to the left of the consonant but is pronounced after it. [3]

The curriculum decision

Use isolated syllables first. Teach the visual contrast, then remove the transliteration from the stimulus. New consonants provide a small transfer task without introducing vocabulary or sentence meaning.

This demo targets visual decoding only. It does not assess pronunciation, listening, handwriting, conjuncts, or connected-word schwa deletion. Human-recorded audio and a broader curriculum would be later additions, following linguistic review.

Why Kannada belongs in the design process

Knowing Kannada gives me a useful comparison point, not permission to assume that another script works identically. A future Kannada companion would begin with an independently reviewed contrast map: what a learner can transfer, what looks similar but behaves differently, and what requires fresh instruction.

I would recruit a Kannada educator for that module, document the transliteration convention, and validate font rendering separately. The present prototype makes no claim to teach Kannada or to represent every Dravidian language.

Proposed assessment and release criteria

Compare matched, unseen syllable sets before and after practice, with stimulus transliteration removed during assessment. Separately record vowel-length confusions, visual-order reversals, and unfamiliar-consonant errors. Add a delayed task to distinguish immediate performance from retention.

Use accessible, readable glyphs and check devices with real learners. If mistakes concentrate on one font or screen size, investigate rendering before adding practice. Do not add automated speech scoring until pronunciation targets, accent variation, and error costs have been reviewed.

Project 03 / Human-in-the-loop content operations

Before It Becomes
a Lesson.

A polished sentence still needs someone to ask what it teaches.

The operational problem

A content pipeline needs more than an approve button. Reviewers need a shared definition of a defect, a place to record disagreement, and a way to prevent unresolved issues from reaching learners.

This workbench makes those decisions visible. Six constructed fixtures demonstrate common review problems and one sound item. They are not a measured failure rate from a model benchmark.

The design decision

Separate six review dimensions: accuracy, context, variation, learning objective, difficulty, and feedback. In this proposed policy, any failed or unresolved dimension blocks approval. The gate is deliberately conservative for a small prototype.

A second educator would independently review a sample and adjudicate disagreements before release. AI can propose or flag material; the accountable reviewer must retain the ability to revise, reject, or ask for more context.

Review operations and scaling plan
  1. Brief: the learning designer fixes objectives, prerequisites, and the target variety before generation.
  2. Draft: record model, prompt version, date, and unedited output; run structural checks for empty keys and duplicate options.
  3. Review: an educator checks all six dimensions. A cultural or regional consultant reviews claims within their expertise.
  4. Adjudicate: preserve disagreements; resolve them against the documented scope rather than majority preference alone.
  5. Release: publish only approved, versioned items. Retain a rollback path and an issue-reporting route.
  6. Monitor: audit error clusters, learner explanations, complaints, and reviewer disagreement. Re-open items when evidence contradicts the key.

Contributor onboarding: use the same small calibration set, request reasons before revealing reference judgments, and discuss disagreements. Recruit expertise relevant to the content rather than assuming a single reviewer represents India. Compensate external contributors and obtain permission before publishing their names.

Measure cost and quality together: reviewer minutes per accepted item, revision cycles, escaped critical defects, and agreement by rubric dimension. No values are reported here because no study has been run.

A research plan, not a victory lap

What would change
my mind?

A delightful demo is a starting point. Before arguing that these experiences improve learning, I would want to see what learners do when the familiar example disappears.

Duolingo’s published method describes practice, personalization, motivation, and testing. Its discussion of Time Spent Learning Well also illustrates why a convenient engagement measure can miss the intended learning behavior. These projects use that distinction as a design question, not as evidence of their own effectiveness. [4] [5]

Current evidence boundary

The browser scores are demonstration-session performance. There are no learner-study results, no Duolingo analytics, and no validated effect sizes. The practice counts and fixture counts describe this artifact only.
