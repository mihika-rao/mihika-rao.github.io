THE ANALYTICS PROBLEM

One source. Thousands of measurements. Several plausible stories.

A bright feature is not automatically a finding. It might be signal, noise, blending, optical depth, geometry, or an artifact of how the data were measured.

I analyzed ALMA Band 7 and Band 8 observations of the protostar CARMA-7 to understand what different molecular signals were tracing. The work combined spectral-line identification, image-cube processing, spatial comparisons, line fitting, rotational-diagram regression, and explicit checks on assumptions.

The transferable part is the workflow: turn ambiguity into measurable hypotheses, build a repeatable pipeline, quantify uncertainty, and only then decide what the data support.

Research taught me to ask a product-analytics question before product analytics did: what evidence would actually change my mind?

UNCERTAINTY IS PART OF THE RESULT

A number without its assumptions is just typography.

01Measurement noise

Channel-level noise and calibration uncertainty enter before any model is fit.

02Line selection

Blended or contaminated transitions can move the slope and therefore the inferred temperature.

03Model assumptions

LTE, optical thinness, beam filling, and a single-temperature approximation all have limits.

04Correlated measurements

Adjacent image pixels are not independent at the synthesized-beam scale.

The point was never to produce the most precise-looking number. It was to know which conclusions were robust enough to keep.

WHAT THE DATA SUPPORTED

Different signals were tracing different physical conditions.

SO₂ was broad and compact near the outflow base; SiO was broader still; complex-organic emission was narrower. The combination supported a picture of recently processed, shock-associated gas rather than one uniform component.

SO₂~14 km/sbroad / disturbed gas
CH₃CN~5–6 km/scompact warm component
SiOup to ~26 km/shigher-velocity shocked gas

The analytics lesson I carried out of astronomy is simple: separate what you observed from what you inferred, and make the assumptions visible enough that someone else can challenge them.
